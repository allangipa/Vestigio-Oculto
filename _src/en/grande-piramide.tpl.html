The empty corridors of the Great Pyramid

It is the most measured monument in history. It has been climbed, mapped, probed, drilled and debated for two centuries. And in 2017 it was discovered that there was a space inside it the size of an airplane, about which not a single line had ever been written.

Cosmic muons

At this very moment, particles generated in the upper atmosphere by the impact of cosmic rays are passing through the Great Pyramid from top to bottom. They are called muons, they weigh two hundred times more than an electron, they last two millionths of a second, and they reach the ground at about ten thousand per square meter per minute. They pass through stone the way light passes through frosted glass: they get through, but part of the flux is lost along the way. The more material, the fewer muons on the other side.

That is the whole idea. If you place a detector inside the pyramid and count, for days on end, how many muons arrive from each direction, the map that forms is an X-ray. Where more muons arrive than the density of the limestone predicts, there is less stone in the way. There is a void.

No one has to open anything. The stone is read from within, without being touched.

01Before ScanPyramids

Until the twentieth century, the inventory of the interior was short and closed: a descending passage, an ascending one, the Queen's Chamber, the Grand Gallery and the King's Chamber — all of them already emptied in antiquity. The last large space added to that list was opened in 1837, and by force. The British colonel Richard Howard Vyse suspected there were more compartments above the King's Chamber, because one of them had been known since 1765, when Nathaniel Davison reached it. Vyse blasted his way upward with gunpowder and found four more. These are the relieving chambers: five low, stacked, undecorated compartments that distribute the weight of a mountain of some six million tons of stone and keep the ceiling of the King's Chamber from giving way. Inside them, the workers left builders' marks painted in red — and one of them bears the cartouche of Khufu.

After 1837, for one hundred and eighty years, no new chamber. The honest exception is tiny: in 2002 a robot drilled a 1.9 cm hole in the slab that closes one of the shafts of the Queen's Chamber, passed a camera through it and saw, behind it, a gap of a few hand-spans and a second slab — it is the same structure as in section 08, and it has nothing to do with the big void.

The first attempt to read a pyramid without opening it came from a physicist. In the mid-1960s, Luis Alvarez — who would win the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1968 — proposed using muons on the pyramid of Khafre, the second at Giza. The Six-Day War, in 1967, interrupted the installation; in 1968 the detectors, set up in Belzoni's chamber, began recording, and the count went on until 1969. The result came out in Science in February 1970: about 19% of the volume examined, and no chamber. The method worked — there was just nothing to find there. Almost half a century later, the same idea would be aimed at Khufu.

02ScanPyramids

The project began in October 2015, run by Cairo University together with the French institute HIP — Heritage Innovation Preservation —, under the direction of Mehdi Tayoubi and Hany Helal. The proposal was explicit and conservative: to examine the pyramids of the Old Kingdom exclusively by methods that did not touch them. Infrared thermography to find temperature differences on the surface, photogrammetry for the three-dimensional model, and muography for the interior.

The first clue appeared in November 2015: infrared thermography recorded, behind the chevrons — the large V-shaped stones that relieve the weight above the original entrance, on the north face —, a thermal behavior that did not match the rest of the face. It was enough for the team to install nuclear emulsion films in the Descending Corridor, aimed at that area. On October 15, 2016, after sixty-seven days of exposure, came the announcement: there was an excess of muons there with a significance of five sigma — the same degree of certainty required to announce the Higgs boson —, that is, an elongated void at least five meters long.

03The big void

On November 2, 2017, Nature published the result that took the subject out of academic circles. Above the Grand Gallery — the ascending passage with a very high ceiling that leads to the King's Chamber — there was a cavity at least thirty meters long, with a cross-section comparable to that of the Gallery itself.

What gives the finding its weight is the design of the experiment. Three teams worked in parallel with different, independent detection technologies: Nagoya University with nuclear emulsion films, the KEK laboratory with scintillators, and the French CEA with gas detectors. Three distinct instruments, three teams that did not share raw data, and the same void in the same place. It is not the reading of a faulty device.

Why "void" and not "chamber" Muography measures density, not shape. It says with confidence that there is less stone there; it does not say whether it is a room, a structural gap, a sequence of separate spaces or a collapsed gallery. Calling the finding a "secret chamber" adds to the data information the data does not have — and that is what much of the coverage did in 2017.

The harshest reaction came from inside. On that same November 2, Zahi Hawass — former Minister of Antiquities and, at the time, head of the scientific committee overseeing ScanPyramids itself — told the New York Times that the team had found nothing and that the article offered nothing to Egyptology. The argument was that the pyramid is full of construction gaps, that this has been known for decades, and that a gap is not a discovery.

The Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities issued a statement the next day saying that the existence of several voids inside the pyramid was nothing new. Two days later, the acting minister said the opposite: that the finding was new and larger than the known cavities. The same government, two answers, in forty-eight hours.

04The north face corridor

The 2016 finding took longer to measure, and ended better. In March 2023, Nature Communications published the precise characterization of the structure behind the chevrons: a horizontal corridor nine meters long with a cross-section of about two by two meters, its floor roughly twenty meters above the base — some seven meters above the original entrance — and less than a meter from the outer face of the chevron.

And this time it was possible to look. An endoscopic camera six millimeters in diameter was inserted through an existing joint between blocks — with no new drilling at all — and sent back images of the interior. The corridor exists, it has a gabled ceiling formed by inclined slabs leaning against each other, and it is empty.

Empty is, in fact, the word that sums up the result. There was no treasure, there was no inscription, there was no sarcophagus. What there was, was architecture: a structure built on purpose, carefully, and then sealed for four thousand five hundred years without anyone writing a line about it.

What came afterward

The corridor did not remain the finding of a single method. In 2025 Scientific Reports published two independent confirmations of the muography: one by fusing three non-destructive techniques — ground-penetrating radar, ultrasound and electrical resistivity tomography — and another devoted to resistivity alone. All of them point to the same structure, in the same place.

And on June 18, 2026 the same team published something different: shear-wave ultrasound on the granite blocks of the King's Chamber. Forty-five blocks were measured, one by one, from the two lower courses of the four walls. The bulk of the result is engineering — the thickness of each block, where the back face ends, the geometry of the joints — and from it came the first three-dimensional model of the inside of that lining.

But in one block of the north wall, identified as NW9, a reflector appeared behind the back face, about two meters deep and running through the entire height of the block. The authors classify it as “a plausible but not uniquely constrained indication of an internal boundary or cavity” — and say the data do not allow them to pin down which of the two.

The same discipline as in 2017 Nine years later, the team still refuses to give an architectural name to what the instrument does not measure. They did not write chamber, they did not write corridor. It is this habit that separates this work from the article the journal had to retract, just below.

05Who built it, and how we know

Before the theories, it is worth establishing what is documented — because this is a case in which the answer exists, and exists in writing.

In 2013, a French mission led by Pierre Tallet, of the Sorbonne, with Gregory Marouard, was excavating at Wadi al-Jarf, an ancient port on Egypt's Red Sea coast. In front of caves used to store dismantled boats, they found papyrus rolls. They are the oldest inscribed papyri known, and they date from the twenty-sixth and twenty-seventh years of Khufu's reign — that is, from the period when the pyramid was being built.

One of them is a logbook. It belonged to an inspector named Merer, who led about forty boatmen. The text records, day by day, the crew's routine: loading blocks of white limestone at the quarries of Tura, on the east bank of the Nile, sailing to Akhet-Khufu — the building site of the Great Pyramid —, unloading and going back. A full cycle took four days. Based on the records, the estimate is something around two hundred blocks a month, of two to three tons each.

The question "how did they build it" has a bureaucratic answer, written by hand, by a mid-ranking official who was there. What the Diary of Merer adds to the case

The diary does not explain everything — it covers the transport of the casing, not the raising of the core blocks, and it is the record of one crew among many. But it destroys an entire premise. There was administration, shifts, accounting, a chain of command and a vizier overseeing the port of Giza, a nobleman named Ankhhaf, half-brother of the pharaoh himself.

The other pillar of the evidence lies on the ground of the plateau: the workers' village, with bakeries, breweries, storehouses and lodgings, and the builders' tombs dug beside the monument. Those buried there received graves with inscriptions, offerings and titles. That is not how enslaved labor is buried.

06The theories, from the most sober to the most improbable

Weight of evidenceHigh

The voids are a structural solution

The most economical reading, and the one the north face corridor seems to confirm. The pyramid already has five relieving chambers stacked above the King's Chamber, whose function is to distribute the weight of the mass above and keep the ceiling from giving way. The chevrons at the entrance do the same job. An empty corridor set against the north face, with a gabled ceiling, has exactly the geometry of something that relieves load.

Applied to the big void above the Gallery, the hypothesis is plausible by the same reasoning — but there the scale is different, and thirty meters is a lot of load relief.

Weight of evidenceDisputed

They are remnants of the construction process

The internal ramp hypothesis, championed mainly by the architect Jean-Pierre Houdin, proposes that the blocks went up by a spiral ramp built inside the mass itself, and that stretches of it remained as voids at the end of the work. The big void would be one of those stretches, or a maneuvering space.

The idea is ingenious and explains well the logistical problem of raising blocks above one hundred meters. What is missing is confirmation: muography has not traced any spiral, and the position of the only large void located so far — on the central axis, above the Gallery — is not the one a spiral ramp would require, even though Houdin maintains the opposite.

Weight of evidenceMinority view

There is an intact chamber with contents

It is the hypothesis that fires the imagination and the one that holds up least so far. The only cavity ever seen from the inside was empty. The known chambers of the pyramid were all emptied in antiquity. And the sarcophagus in the King's Chamber was already without its lid and without contents when the first modern records describe it.

It is not impossible — the big void has never been observed. But the expectation of treasure says more about those who expect it than about the pyramid.

Weight of evidenceNone

Lost technology, hidden energy, non-human builders

This is the thesis that the Diary of Merer settles on its own. There is a contemporary record, written by an official at the building site, describing the transport routine. There is the village of those who worked, with its bread ovens. There are the builders' tombs with their names. There are the work-gang marks painted on blocks inside the relieving chambers, with the name of the crew and the year of the reign.

What this reading does is take the credit away from a people who left their own accounting in writing and hand it to imaginary agents. The real feat — organizing and feeding thousands of people for two decades, with a working bureaucracy — is more impressive than any mystical substitute, and it is documented.

Weight of evidenceRetracted

A network of tunnels seen by satellite radar

In October 2022, Filippo Biondi and Corrado Malanga published in Remote Sensing a method that promised to reconstruct the interior of the pyramid from space: measuring micro-movements caused by background seismic vibration, picked up by satellite synthetic-aperture radar, and from them drawing what lies inside the stone. What they claimed to have found was a three-dimensional network of ramps, rooms and tunnels far more extensive than anything known.

It was this line of work that fed, in 2025, the wave of videos about a gigantic city hidden beneath the Giza plateau.

On August 10, 2026 the journal retracted the article, citing serious methodological flaws and statistical errors that invalidated the conclusions. A retraction is not a correction: it is the formal invalidation of the article, which remains accessible, but stamped as retracted. The central objection, made by the specialist in radar applied to archaeology Lawrence Conyers, of the University of Denver, is simple — the signal from that satellite has no way of penetrating the pyramid, reflecting off internal structures and returning to the sensor, and the vibrations the method claimed to measure are far below what is detectable from orbit.

The retraction does not erase the voids. The big void of 2017 and the corridor of 2023 still stand, measured by other methods, by other teams, and later confirmed by three independent techniques. What fell was the underground city.

08What remains open

The big void of 2017 has never been observed. It is known to be there, its approximate location and minimum size are known, and what it is is not known. It sits in the middle of the mass, far from any accessible joint, and reaching it would require opening the stone.

This is where the case is decided. The pyramid is not a site that can be excavated and redone: every intervention is permanent. The choice of ScanPyramids was not to touch, and the 2023 corridor showed that the choice works — it was possible to see a space sealed for four millennia through a crack that already existed.

Three questions remain unanswered. What the big void is — a room, a gap, a collapsed gallery or several spaces that muography reads as one. How many others exist — the method's resolution improves with each generation of detector, and small cavities may lie below what the 2017 instruments could distinguish. Whether there is a plan behind them — whether the voids form a coherent load-relieving system, or whether they are building-site accidents accumulated over twenty years of work.

What was promised, and has not come yet In November 2025, at an international book fair in the United Arab Emirates, Zahi Hawass announced that a discovery inside the Great Pyramid would be revealed in 2026 and that it would rewrite a chapter of the history of the pharaohs. He described a thirty-meter passage ending at a door, reached by robots.

It is worth separating what is publication from what is statement. No peer-reviewed article describes a door at the end of the big void, and no one has gotten there. A robot with a camera did in fact find a stone slab closing a narrow shaft, with two copper handles — but that was in 1993, in one of the shafts of the Queen's Chamber, by Rudolf Gantenbrink's team, and it is a different structure. In 2002 another robot drilled a 1.9 cm hole in that slab, passed a camera through it and saw, a few hand-spans away, a second slab; in 2011 the robot Djedi, from the University of Leeds, filmed marks in red ochre there. As of September 2026, no report and no announcement.
The pattern running through this archive Göbekli Tepe is 90% unexcavated. Less than 1% of the Amazon has been swept by lidar. The Denisovans were identified through the DNA of a fragment no one had looked at twice, and later in fossils kept in drawers. And the Great Pyramid was read by particles that come from space, without anyone touching it. In none of the four cases was the obstacle a lack of material: the material was always there. What changed was the existence of the instrument — and, in this case, the decision to use one that does not destroy.

09Frequently asked questions

Where is the Great Pyramid of Giza?

On the Giza plateau, in Egypt. It was built around 2560 BC for the pharaoh Khufu, in the Fourth Dynasty.

How tall is the Great Pyramid?

Its original height was 146.6 meters.

What is inside the Great Pyramid?

The known interior is a descending passage, an ascending one, the Queen's Chamber, the Grand Gallery, the King's Chamber and the five relieving chambers above it, all emptied since antiquity. Muography added an empty corridor on the north face and a big void above the Gallery. See before ScanPyramids.

Is there a secret chamber in the Great Pyramid?

In 2017, three independent teams used muons to detect a void at least thirty meters long above the Grand Gallery. Muography measures density, not shape: no one knows whether it is a room, a gap or a collapsed gallery, and the void has never been observed. See the big void.

Who built the Great Pyramid?

Egyptian crews organized by Khufu's administration. The diary of the inspector Merer records the transport of limestone from Tura to the building site, and the workers' village and the builders' tombs, with inscriptions and offerings, are on the plateau itself. See who built it, and how we know.
